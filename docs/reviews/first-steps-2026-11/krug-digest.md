# Krug, Don't Make Me Think, Revisited: digest for a first-visit and tutorial flow

Citations give the PDF page index (as passed to Read), then [print page] from the book's own index where it lists one. The PDF has no printed page numbers and the offset drifts (about 19 at Chapter 1, 39 at Chapter 12), so use the pairs given. Ch.1-7 and 10-13 read in full; 8-9 for instructions and testing.

## 1. Principles

1. **Obvious beats clever.** Cute, marketing-led or insider names make people stop and wonder, so lean plain. The book's headline rule: "Don't make me think!" Ch.1, PDF 29 [10-11], 32-33 [14]. Page titles should echo the label tapped, PDF 107 [76]. *Apply:* gloss critter, burrow, scatter and feast in plain words at first contact.

2. **Self-evident, else self-explanatory.** If it can't be obvious, look, names and a little crafted text must make the effort tiny. Ch.1, PDF 37. *Apply:* one glanceable sentence per screen for secret scattering and the simultaneous reveal.

3. **People scan; format for it.** Visitors hunt for words matching their goal and skip the rest, so use headings, short paragraphs, lists, sparing bold. Ch.2-3, PDF 40-41 [22-23], 63-68. *Apply:* each intro screen survives one glance on a phone, key word first.

4. **People satisfice and muddle through.** They take the first plausible option, rarely read instructions, hold wrong theories of how things work and don't care to know; builders who enjoy mechanisms find this hard to believe. Ch.2, PDF 42-46 [24-27]; Ch.8, PDF 137. *Apply:* make the next action unmistakable; keep encryption detail optional.

5. **Conventions; clarity over consistency.** Familiar patterns cost nothing to learn; depart only when clearly better, and give up a little consistency for a lot of clarity. Ch.3, PDF 48-53 [29-33], 56. *Apply:* standard connect, continue and back patterns; delight rides on plain controls.

6. **Hierarchy and noise.** Importance should show through prominence, grouping and nesting; shouting, disorder and clutter bury the signal, and animation distracts some. Ch.3, PDF 56-63 [33-38]. *Apply:* one focal element per screen; art, motion and music must not compete with the next step.

7. **Mindless, unambiguous choices.** Tap count matters less than the thought each costs; a strong scent of information keeps people confident; a first choice can open only the relevant details; asking visitors to classify themselves stalls them. Ch.4, PDF 70-72 [42-47]. *Apply:* several trivially clear first steps beat one dense screen.

8. **Brief, timely, unavoidable guidance.** For a hard choice give the least help needed, at the moment of need, where it can't be missed (London's curb-painted look-right warning). Ch.4, PDF 75. *Apply:* explain the scatter-everything rule at the scatter control.

9. **Omit needless words; kill happy talk.** Halve the words, then halve again: it quiets the page and removes the implied chore of reading; welcome fluff conveys nothing. Ch.5, PDF 77-78 [48-50]. *Apply:* a word budget per intro screen; no greeting that doesn't say what or how.

10. **Instructions: eliminate, else minimise, at the point of action.** Nobody reads them until muddling fails; make the interface explain itself and cut the survivors. Ch.5, PDF 78-80 [51-52]. *Apply:* hold closing-time details for the step that needs them.

11. **Navigation is the instructions.** It tells people what's here, where to begin and their options, and earns trust in the makers. The web lacks scale, direction and location cues, so supply them, with an obvious reset; subtle cues get missed. Ch.6, PDF 91-93, 101, 109; form-like steps want stripped-down navigation, PDF 97 [67]. *Apply:* show step n of m and a visible exit.

12. **Big picture first, then where to start.** Visitors must learn what this is, what they can do, what's here and why here; snap impressions predict later judgments and a wrong first guess compounds. Then comes an obvious, plainly labelled start. Ch.7, PDF 119-120 [89], 128-129. *Apply:* the invite screen answers all of it in a sentence or two, above one primary button.

13. **Arriving mid-stream.** Many land from a message link and hunt for bearings; the trunk test asks whether a random page answers where you are, at arm's length. Ch.6-7, PDF 112-113 [82-83], 121-122 [92]. *Apply:* the invite link is the entire first impression; the landing screen must stand alone and name the burrow and inviter.

14. **Tagline, welcome blurb, learn-more.** Three places people expect a statement of purpose: a short phrase by the name, a terse block at the top, a pointer to fuller explanation. Ch.7, PDF 122-127 [93-98]. *Apply:* one line under the wordmark, with a quiet learn-more route.

15. **Affordances without hover.** Touch has no cursor, so tooltips and hover cues vanish; flat styling then drops the cues that tell a button from a heading. Ch.3, PDF 60, 62; Ch.10, PDF 187-189 [151-53]. *Apply:* no hover-only help; minimal or painterly elements must still look tappable.

16. **Mobile is tradeoffs.** Urgent and frequent things in reach, the rest a few taps away on an obvious path; extra taps are fine while confidence lasts; slow loads cost goodwill. Ch.10, PDF 177-178 [145-47], 183-185 [147-50], 189-190. *Apply:* keep first-screen weight low; load big art and explainers afterwards.

17. **Tours are hard to refind and easy to forget.** First-launch slides are often impossible to find again, and even a polished ten-screen tour plus a learn-by-doing list left the author's demo volunteers unable to do the app's main task. Ch.10, PDF 193-196 [155-59]. *Apply:* teach inside the real flow (a practice scatter); keep a permanent how-it-works entry.

18. **Goodwill is a leaky reservoir.** Hiding wanted information, asking for unneeded data, marketing sizzle and long forms drain it, and one slip can empty it; candour, saved steps, honest FAQs, easy recovery and apology refill it. Ch.11, PDF 204-211 [166-71]. *Apply:* say plainly what is hidden, from whom, until when; ask only what the next step needs.

19. **Accessibility basics.** Fix what confuses everyone first; screen-reader users scan by ear, so front-load key words; real headings, labels, contrast and scalable type matter. Ch.12, PDF 212, 219-222 [173-81]; Ch.13, PDF 237. *Apply:* check copy at large type and in both colour modes; no labels living inside fields.

20. **Test with fresh eyes; subtract before adding.** Makers stop seeing their own work; three testers per round find plenty; a first-impression tour asks what they think the page is for. Faced with confusion the reflex is to add explanation, yet removing what obscures often works better. Ch.9, PDF 144, 150, 158, 162, 169-170 [139]. *Apply:* put the invite link before three newcomers on real phones.

## 2. Instructions and tutorials, specifically

- **The section on killing instructions** (PDF 78 [51-52]): nobody reads them until repeated muddling has failed, and wordy ones defeat even then. Eliminate them by making the design explain itself; cut any survivors to the bare minimum.
- **Chapter 5's worked example** (PDF 79-80 [51-52]) trims survey instructions from 103 to 34 words. The margin notes (figure, PDF 80) squeeze the opener to a bare ask, drop the how-to-fill-a-form line (those who need it won't know the jargon), keep the 2-3 minute estimate (useful while deciding whether to bother), and move a late-stage instruction to where it can be acted on.
- **Why people skip:** they are on a mission, need only a fraction, and are practised scanners (PDF 41); they don't care how things work (PDF 46); anything that looks like a big time investment is less likely to be used (footnote, PDF 25).
- **When they read:** after failing (PDF 78); when help is brief, timely and unavoidable (PDF 75); with document-like content (PDF 40); for a novel idea, where a short video is now accepted (PDF 123); a new app owner will spend a little time at once to learn it (PDF 198).

## 3. What a first page must answer

- **Four questions** (figure, PDF 119): what is this; what can I do here; what do they have here; why here rather than elsewhere. A **fifth** (PDF 128-129): where do I start, with sign-in entry points prominent and plain labels such as *Start here* for step-by-step processes.
- **Why:** the big picture is what committees drop, because nobody lobbies for it (PDF 119-120). Four excuses are rebutted (figure, PDF 121): it's obvious to us; repetition annoys (only if it fills half the page); real users will know; advertising covers it.
- **Tagline** (PDF 125-128): six to eight words, clear, so specific only you could use it, a benefit not a motto; cleverness only if it carries the benefit.
- **Welcome blurb** (PDF 123-125): terse, prominent, top left or centre; as much space as needed and no more; not a mission statement; test it on outsiders.
- **Learn-more** (PDF 123-124): a short video is now expected and accepted; Kickstarter makes its what-is-it item the most prominent in navigation and invites the curious from its hero. Most people try to guess first; it exists for those who can't.

## 4. Mobile, for a phone-first flow

- Same principles; people move faster and read even less on small screens (PDF 176), and the chapter avoids prescribing patterns.
- **No hover, flat styling:** affordances are the last thing to hide (PDF 187); clickability is resurfacing as a mobile problem (PDF 62). The book does not set small screen against flat design: touch removes hover, and flat styling strips visual distinctions further, so use the dimensions that remain (PDF 188-189).
- **Deep links:** a tap from email or social should reach the thing linked, not a front page (PDF 185-186); always offer a route to the full site (PDF 186).
- **Priorities:** mobile users eventually want everything, not a subset (PDF 183-184); don't trade usability for screen space (PDF 185).
- **Speed:** slow loads cost goodwill and networks vary (PDF 189-190). **Forms:** labels inside fields only under strict conditions (PDF 237). **Testing:** film screen and fingers, not a mirror (PDF 199-200).

## 5. Going deeper without forcing it

The book has no section on progressive disclosure by that name. Closest material:

- A learn-more pointer for those who can't guess (PDF 123); a first choice that reveals only the relevant details (PDF 72); on-demand what's-this links (PDF 75); options shown when useful, such as search scope on the results page (PDF 102-103); honest FAQs rather than marketing posing as questions (PDF 210).
- **Second routes:** Harlem.org pairs a playful photo navigation with ordinary navigation because the fun may wear off (PDF 54-56); mobile sites keep a full-site link (PDF 186).
- **Audiences:** experts are rarely insulted by clarity aimed at beginners (PDF 152).
- **Tradeoffs:** the answer usually depends (PDF 237); bend rules knowingly (PDF 238); subtract before adding (PDF 169-170).
- **Silent on:** onboarding or tutorial design as such, and on explaining cryptography or earning trust in hidden mechanisms; nearest are candour and goodwill (PDF 207-210) and first-page credibility (PDF 117).
