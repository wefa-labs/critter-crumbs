# Schell, The Art of Game Design: digest for a screen-by-screen audit

Citations give the PDF page index (as passed to Read). The copy is the first edition (2008): 32 chapters and 100 numbered lenses, cited here by Schell's number so a finding can name its lens. The printed page is PDF − 31 throughout the body (PDF 145 is printed 114, PDF 255 is printed 224, both checked against the rendered page), so the book's own lens list (PDF 18-20) converts by adding 31. Ch.2-5, 8-9, 12-14, 16-17 and 20 read in full; 10, 11 and 18 except the probability rules, the economy section and the character-transformation tail; 6-7, 15, 19, 21-22 and 30-31 for the lenses named below. The book is about games to be good at; §3 collects the places where Schell himself says when not to reach for those patterns, and §4 what this edition does not have.

## 1. Principles, chapter by chapter

**Ch.2, the designer creates an experience**

1. **The game is not the experience.** The designer builds an artifact and hopes the right experience rises from it; the experience lives in the player, cannot be seen or shared, and is the only thing that matters: "The game is not the experience." PDF 41-42. Games earn their trouble through feelings only they give: choice, freedom, responsibility, accomplishment, friendship. PDF 43. *Apply:* audit the feeling each screen produces, not the screen; say which of those five the scatter and the feast are for.

2. **Dissect your feelings; two passes.** A designer must say what is wrong and why, not merely that it was bad; analyse memories, run the experience once untouched and once stopping to take notes, or learn to glance at it without breaking it. PDF 48-50. *Apply:* each reviewer runs a feast twice on a phone, the first time without notes.

3. **Lens #1, essential experience.** Pick the few features that define the experience you want and find many ways to put them in; Wii baseball dropped nine innings to get the swing right. PDF 51-52. The only reality is what is felt, so a designed experience can feel as real as life. PDF 52-53. *Apply:* Game Design §1 is the written essential experience (feeding not filling in, a reply on the same frame, weight without spectacle, quiet then one big moment, no sad critters); grade every screen on whether it carries that or dilutes it.

**Ch.3, the experience rises out of a game**

4. **Toy, fun, play, game.** A toy is an object you play with; a good toy is fun to play with; fun is pleasure with surprises; play is manipulation that indulges curiosity; a game is problem solving approached playfully, and once the problem is gone "it is no longer a game, just an activity". Lens #2 Surprise (what surprises players, can they surprise each other), #3 Fun, #4 Curiosity (what questions does the game put in the player's mind, how do I make them care). PDF 57-61, 65-68. *Apply:* by these definitions Critter Crumbs is a toy with a deadline: the problem it serves (how the pot is split) is the council's real one. Judge it with the toy and curiosity lenses, never the challenge ones; its curiosity is what my friends wrote and what the feast will show.

5. **Lens #5, endogenous value.** What is valuable inside the game measures how much players care; Bubsy's yarn balls were ignored because points bought nothing, Sonic's rings mattered because they protected you, roulette needs real money because the game makes no value of its own. PDF 63-64. *Apply:* crumbs are real money, so their value is already there; do not add in-app value (scores, badges, a best-scatterer mark), it would be yarn balls.

6. **Work is obligation, play is freedom.** The same activity is work when you must and play when you choose; Rico turned an assembly line into play with a personal question; whoever must play cannot play. PDF 59-61. *Apply:* a council member has to scatter. The app cannot make that voluntary, only unforced: no nagging counters, no guilt copy, one quiet reminder of the close.

**Ch.4, the game consists of elements**

7. **Lens #7, the elemental tetrad.** Mechanics, story, aesthetics and technology are equal and each bends for the others; Space Invaders got colour from strips of plastic and a heartbeat that sped up as the aliens did. PDF 72-76. *Apply:* every screen has all four: the rule it enforces, the moment in the story (gathering, scattering, feast), the look and sound, and the technical state it shows or hides (loading, 3D ready, decrypting). A finding names the quadrant.

8. **Lens #8, holographic design.** See skin and skeleton at once: which elements make the experience enjoyable, which detract, what to change. PDF 76-77. *Apply:* no finding says only that something feels wrong; it says which element and why.

**Ch.5, the elements support a theme**

9. **Lens #9, unification.** Decide what the game is about, then reinforce it with everything: the elephant book cut into an elephant shape, air vents placed to blow like sea breeze, net bags for your things on the pirate ship; many theming details are a line of text, a colour, a sound; the test is "If it reinforces the theme, it stays, but if it doesn't, it goes." PDF 80-84. *Apply:* the theme is a burrow at dusk where you feed small creatures. Audit every piece of chrome (sheets, buttons, errors, loading, settings, the connect step) for whether it belongs in the burrow or in a generic web app.

10. **Lens #10, resonance.** The strongest themes touch hidden wishes (the pirate fantasy is freedom) or hidden truths; players cannot state them but feel them. PDF 84-87. *Apply:* the truth under this tool is that the burrow decides together and nobody is shamed in front of the others; check the reveal and the non-scatterer notice against it.

**Ch.6-7, idea and iteration**

11. **Lens #12, the problem statement.** State the problem before solutions; ask whether a game is really the best solution and how you will know it is solved. PDF 91-93. *Apply:* each audit finding states the problem it saw before its fix.

12. **Lens #13, the eight filters.** Does it feel right, will the audience like it, is it well designed, is it novel, will it sell, can it be built, does it meet social goals, do testers enjoy it. PDF 108-110. *Apply:* the audit covers filters one to three and seven; eight is the rehearsal on real phones.

13. **Lens #15, the toy; build the toy first.** Is it fun with no goal at all; do people want to touch it before they know what to do; Lemmings and Grand Theft Auto were toys before they were games. PDF 121-122. *Apply:* the heap and a critter should invite a tap before any instruction; a screen that only works once read has failed this lens.

**Ch.8, the game is made for a player**

14. **Lens #16, the player.** Project yourself into the audience (Walt Disney crouched to a child's eye height); adults from twenty-five to fifty play casually and their time is precious; to reach someone, speak the games of their childhood. PDF 130-133, 137. *Apply:* the council are busy adults in a group-chat lull, so a whole scatter must fit a lull; the cosy console-game sound is a childhood language, and that is its justification.

15. **Pleasures split by temperament.** Mastery, competition, destruction, spatial puzzles and trial-and-error on one side; emotion, the real world, nurturing, verbal play and learning by example on the other; Hasbro found competition stressed girls' friendships enough to stop play. PDF 134-136. *Apply:* feeding is nurturing and the money is real, so the tool already sits on the inclusive side; keep competition out of the results (G3: the feast feeds in grid order, never by rank) and keep a worked example for those who learn that way.

16. **Lens #17, pleasure.** LeBlanc's eight: sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission; and more: anticipation, gift giving, possibility, pride, purification, surprise, thrill, wonder. PDF 140-143. *Apply:* this tool's pleasures are sensation, fellowship, anticipation, gift giving (a scatter is a gift), discovery (the statements), purification (emptying the heap) and wonder (the feast); challenge is absent on purpose. Name the pleasure each screen serves; a screen serving none is a form.

**Ch.9, the experience is in the player's mind**

17. **Modeling.** Minds run on simplified models; cartoons soothe because they are pre-digested; games relax because so much complexity is stripped away. PDF 146-149. *Apply:* simple critters and few rules are the point; every extra state a person must hold (phases, closes, who has done what) is a cost to justify.

18. **Lens #18, focus and flow.** Flow needs clear goals, no distractions, direct feedback and continuous challenge inside a channel between boredom and anxiety; tense and release. PDF 149-153. *Apply:* three of the four hold (goal: empty the heap; nothing competing for attention; a reply on the same frame); the fourth is deliberately missing, so a scatter must be short and the music must not try to hold people longer.

19. **Empathy.** We take on the feelings of faces we see, even drawn ones; dogs evolved expressive faces to capture it. PDF 154-155. *Apply:* this is why a critter never drops below calm (G4); audit any face, idle pose or results pose that a tired person could read as sad or disapproving.

20. **Imagination.** Players fill gaps on their own; the art is knowing what to show and what to leave. PDF 155-157. *Apply:* do not explain the burrow; a few relevant details and the person supplies the rest.

21. **Lens #19 needs and #20 judgment.** Games mostly serve self-esteem; people do not hate being judged, "they only hate being judged unfairly"; ask what the game judges, how it says so, whether it feels fair, whether they care, whether it makes them want to improve. PDF 157-159. *Apply:* the feast is a judgment by friends with real money. The results must read as fair (proportions of crumbs actually scattered, non-scatterers named plainly) and must not invite improvement: there is nothing to get better at, so nothing on the results screen may look like a score to beat.

**Ch.10, game mechanics**

22. **Lens #22, dynamic state and secrets.** Decide who knows which state (public, shared, private, known only to the game); making private information suddenly public is a way to create drama; "if two objects behave the same way, they should look the same". PDF 167-171. *Apply:* the whole tool is built on that drama: scatters private until the feast, statements hidden until scattering opens. Every screen should make plain what is hidden and from whom; things that behave differently (my own critter, a budget option, a non-scatterer) must look different.

23. **Lens #24, action.** Few verbs acting on many objects give elegant emergence; players notice the verbs they wish they had. PDF 171-175. *Apply:* the verbs are tap, hold, take back, type the exact number, *Scatter these*. Each must work on every critter and feel the same (F4: minus as good as plus).

24. **Lens #25 goals and #26 rules.** The most important rule is the object of the game stated simply (chess is "Capture your opponent's king", not a speech about check); goals must be concrete, achievable and rewarding, balanced between short and long; written rules go unread, the game must teach, so every designer must answer how players will learn; computers turn rules into physical constraints to be discovered rather than memorised; too many modes confuse, and nobody should stay in a sub-game long enough to forget the main one. PDF 177-181. *Apply:* each screen states its one goal in one line; the constraints (all crumbs must go, none to yourself) are enforced by the heap and the controls, not by error text afterwards.

25. **Lens #27, skill.** Real skills against virtual ones; virtual skill pushed too far feels hollow; a game thought to be about one skill is often about another. PDF 181-184. *Apply:* the only real skill asked is stopping a pour where you meant (F2); nothing else may demand dexterity, and the pour must stay controllable on the slowest phone in the burrow.

**Ch.11, balance**

26. **Lens #30, fairness.** Players universally want fairness; symmetry (equal resources and powers) is the simplest way to give it. PDF 203-204, 207. *Apply:* equal crumbs is the symmetry; show it (the same handful for everyone, visibly) and keep the results legible as proportions.

27. **Lens #31, challenge against success.** Designers harden later levels to stretch play and lose most players; learning to play is itself a challenge, so early success builds confidence. PDF 208-210. *Apply:* the first steps and the first scatter must succeed at once with no way to fail.

28. **Lens #32, meaningful choices.** Choices must change what happens; a dominant strategy ends the fun; more choices than desires overwhelms, fewer frustrates, equal feels like freedom. PDF 210-212. *Apply:* the scatter is the one meaningful choice and the critter count is the number of choices; with ten critters, check that the running split and the remaining heap keep the choice graspable.

29. **Lens #33, triangularity.** A safe small reward against a risky big one is what most dull prototypes are missing. PDF 212-214. *Apply:* scattering has no risk and no reward, so there is no triangularity to balance; do not manufacture any.

30. **Lens #39, time.** Too short frustrates, too long bores; a time limit can add excitement; "Leave 'em wanting more." PDF 219-220. *Apply:* the creator sets the gathering and scattering windows and the feast plays under eight seconds (F8); the wait between a scatter and the feast is designed time too and needs its own screen.

31. **Lens #40, reward.** Praise, points, prolonged play, gateway, spectacle, expression, powers, resources, completion; people acclimatise, so games inflate rewards and make them variable, which Schell calls a cheesy trick that works; "Getting a reward you don't understand is like getting no reward at all." PDF 220-222. *Apply:* allowed here: praise (the small ta-da when the heap empties), spectacle (the feast) and completion. No points, no variable schedule, nothing that builds across feasts.

32. **Lens #41, punishment.** Shaming is a punishment; reward beats punishment (Diablo turned hunger into a food bonus); punishment must be for things a player can understand and prevent, or the game is called unfair. PDF 223-225. *Apply:* telling the burrow who did not scatter is a shaming punishment the PRD requires; keep it factual, quiet and preventable (the close visible everywhere, a reminder sent). The line that a scatter can be changed until the close turns the deadline from threat to reassurance.

33. **Freedom against controlled experience.** Take control where everyone wants the same thing and nobody notices (the flight up to Jafar). PDF 226. *Apply:* the feast plays itself; *Skip to results* keeps the feeling of freedom.

34. **Lens #42 simplicity, #43 elegance, #44 character.** Prefer emergent to innate complexity; rate an element by how many purposes it serves; when something feels wrong ask what to remove rather than what to add; but keep the quirks, since "Elegance and character are opposites." PDF 226-230. *Apply:* the heap serves five purposes (input target, progress, signature, theme, legibility); count purposes for every other element and remove the one-purpose chrome; keep the oddities (critters grown from addresses, the mouth arc).

35. **Lens #45, detail against imagination.** "Only detail what you can do well"; give details the imagination can reuse (chess pieces as a court); the binocular effect, a close-up early so small figures stay vivid. PDF 230-232. *Apply:* portraits first, 3D later is this rule; a close-up of your own critter early (invite, first steps) lets grid-size critters stay characters.

**Ch.12, puzzles, where the accessibility and progress lenses live**

36. **Lens #48, accessibility.** Make the goal obvious at a glance and the first move obvious; players "should be able to clearly visualize what their first few steps would be"; a good puzzle starts as a good toy that people want to touch. PDF 242-244. *Apply:* on every screen, can a newcomer see what to do first without reading?

37. **Lens #49, visible progress and solvability.** Progress gives hope; riddles are walls; Rubik's cube ships solved so you know it can be. PDF 244-245. *Apply:* the heap is progress; the waiting screens need progress too (how many have scattered, time left) and the feast must promise from its first frame that it will resolve.

38. **Lens #50 parallelism, #51 pyramid.** Several related tasks let a stuck player rest; small pieces feeding one final point hold interest. PDF 247-248. *Apply:* reading statements and scattering may interleave; the whole feast is a pyramid with the feast at its point.

**Ch.13, the interface, in full**

39. **Lens #53, control.** The goal of an interface is "to make players feel in control of their experience": does it do what is expected, is it easy to master, do players feel they influence the outcome; feeling powerful equals feeling in control. PDF 253-254. *Apply:* every input on the scatter screen does the expected thing on the same frame; nothing moves a crumb the person did not move.

40. **Six mappings.** Player, physical input and output, a virtual interface layer (scores, menus, cameras) and the world, joined by six arrows each of which is code: input to world, world to output, input to virtual, virtual to world, world to virtual, virtual to output; confusion lives on one of the six. PDF 254-256. *Apply:* when a screen confuses, say which arrow: the heap and critters are the world, counts and the sticky bar are the virtual layer, the phone is the physical one.

41. **Lens #54 physical and #55 virtual interface.** What does the player touch and can it please; does it map directly; the virtual layer should give only information not obvious from the world, when it is needed, without getting in the way. PDF 257-258. *Apply:* thumb on the heap is the physical interface; ask of every counter, label and bar what it says that the critters and the heap do not already show.

42. **Lens #56, transparency.** Players project themselves and forget the interface until it confuses them; "The ideal interface becomes invisible to the player"; does it let them do what they want, work without thinking, stay usable under stress and in corner cases. PDF 258-259. *Apply:* watch for the moments a person notices the app: a sheet, the connect step, a loading state.

43. **Lens #57, feedback.** The loop of interaction is a waterwheel; the Swiffer's dirty cloth makes the job feel done (less feedback, dirtier floor); at every moment ask what players need to know, want to know, should feel, want to feel, and what their goal is. PDF 259-261. *Apply:* run the five questions on each screen state, including the dull ones (waiting, closed, error).

44. **The tenth of a second.** An interface that does not respond "within a tenth of a second" feels broken; a quarter-second jump wind-up drove players mad though it was good animation; the British crossing button's WAIT light turns anxiety into confidence. PDF 262-263. *Apply:* the same-frame rule in Game Design §1 is stricter than Schell's; the audit also needs the WAIT light for saves and the network: an immediate acknowledgement, then the quiet confirmation.

45. **Lens #58, juiciness.** Second-order motion caused by the player, rewarding many ways at once; a dry interface contradicts a game meant to be fun; "Juicy interfaces are fun the moment you pick them up." PDF 263-265. *Apply:* a tap gives count, hop, squash, chew and bip at once, which is juicy; check that minus, the exact-number field and the final scatter are as juicy as the tap.

46. **Lens #59, channels and dimensions.** List the information, rank it (always, glance, occasionally), list channels (screen areas, the avatar, sound, music), map one to the other, then use dimensions (size, colour, font) either to reinforce one message or to carry several; Zelda gave health a third of the dashboard. PDF 265-269. *Apply:* do the listing for the scatter screen: heap left, this critter's crumbs, time to close, who has not scattered yet; decide which is always visible and which is a glance.

47. **Lens #60, modes.** Use as few as possible, never overlapping on the same control, and make them look as different as possible; vi is the warning. PDF 269-271. *Apply:* gathering, scattering, closed and feast are modes; each must look unmistakably different at a glance, and the same control must not mean different things across them.

48. **Other interface tips.** Steal a familiar pattern or build bottom-up; theme the interface with the unification lens; "the human mind easily maps sound to touch", so sound stands in for the missing touch; balance options and simplicity with layers; a metaphor explains a delay (a visible radio signal for network lag); test early on paper; break genre rules to help the player. PDF 272-275. *Apply:* the bips and the pour are the touch; a visible metaphor for the network save would do what the radio signal did.

**Ch.14, interest curves**

49. **Lens #61, the interest curve.** Hook, settle, rising interest with rests, climax, resolution; leave them wanting more; a flat stretch is cut, not padded; the pattern is fractal. PDF 277-284. *Apply:* draw the curve of one feast: invite (hook), gathering (rest), reading statements (rise), scatter (peak), wait (rest), feast (climax), results (resolution). Then draw it for one scatter session alone.

50. **Lens #62 inherent interest, #63 beauty, #64 projection.** Interest is inherent interest plus poetry of presentation plus projection; Tetris has little of the first two and lives on projection; a consistent world sustains projection and one contradiction breaks it. PDF 284-288. *Apply:* the inherent interest is real money and friends' words; the poetry is the six words; the projection is your own critter; audit any contradiction (a critter that behaves out of character, a generic dialog).

**Ch.15, story, one lens bears**

51. **Lens #67, simplicity and transcendence.** Game worlds are simpler than life and the player more powerful in them; a modern setting needs some added power; keep the world consistent, since one contradiction breaks it for good; what players will believe beats what is accurate. PDF 302-304, 307-308. *Apply:* the burrow is simpler than a budget meeting, and the one transcendence is that your crumbs land exactly where you decide, privately, with no discussion.

**Ch.16, indirect control**

52. **Lens #71, freedom.** When do players feel free, when constrained, where is there too much freedom; the feeling of freedom matters, not true freedom. PDF 315. *Apply:* the scatter should feel free (any split you like) while the rules quietly constrain (all of it, none to yourself).

53. **Lens #72, indirect control, six methods.** Constraints (a short list of flavours sells more candy than every flavour), goals (the etched fly), interface (a plastic guitar silently removes other options, and so does the avatar), visual design (the castle as a weenie, the red line nobody remembered following), characters (the Happy Room Academy, Ico's princess), music (restaurants speed or slow eating); collusion makes characters serve the experience; the best leader's people say "We did it ourselves!" PDF 316-329. *Apply:* the strongest audit tool here: on each screen, what does the eye land on first and does that lead to the one action; what does the interface silently forbid; what does the music ask people to do.

**Ch.17, worlds**

54. **Lens #74, the world.** Transmedia worlds are entered through many gateways, each of which must make sense alone; they are intuitive, wish-fulfilling and outlive any one product. PDF 334-338. *Apply:* the burrow persists across feasts; the invite link, the group-chat message, a results screenshot and the app are gateways; each must stand alone and agree with the others.

**Ch.18, characters**

55. **Lens #75, the avatar.** Players project into an ideal form or a blank slate; iconic, simple faces invite projection; people play to be who they wish to be, not themselves. PDF 343-345. Character function: list the roles and fold them together. PDF 346-347. "status is something you do", shown in posture, eye contact and who holds the centre. PDF 352-355. The face is the most expressive channel and worth the polygons. PDF 355-356. *Apply:* a critter is both iconic and a real person; no critter may out-rank another in posture or placement (the host's is not bigger); the expression range is the status language and it stays between calm and delighted.

**Ch.19, spaces**

56. **Lens #82 inner contradiction, #83 the nameless quality.** Landmarks make a space memorable; Alexander's living structures feel alive, whole, comfortable, free, exact and free of inner contradictions; a thing meant to be easy that is hard, or meant to be fun that is dull, contradicts itself; fifteen properties including strong centres, gradients, roughness, simplicity and inner calm, not-separateness. PDF 364-369. *Apply:* the six words are an inner-calm list; the contradiction test is the audit's sharpest: a tool meant to feel quiet must not blink, shout or ask twice.

**Ch.20, aesthetics**

57. **The value of aesthetics; Lens #63 again.** Art draws people in, makes the world feel solid, is a reward in itself and buys forgiveness for flaws; learn to see; pick the music first and let it channel the design; good audio makes the same graphics look better; detail where people look close (Cinderella's castle holds up at arm's length), "distant mountains" elsewhere. PDF 378-383. *Apply:* the music brief is the theme in sound; check that what is seen close (your critter, the heap) is finished and the rest is suggested.

**Ch.21-22, other players**

58. **Why we play with others.** Competition, collaboration, meeting up, exploring our friends (an hour of play shows more than a year of talk), exploring ourselves; friendship needs ways to meet, break the ice and stay in touch; self-expression makes players feel proud and connected. PDF 385-387, 392-394. *Apply:* the feast is meeting up and exploring friends; the statement is the one expressive channel and should feel like one, not a form field.

**Ch.30-31, transformation and responsibility**

59. **Games change people; keep them in a balanced life.** Experiences are the only things that change people; Toontown's polite phrase menu changed a player's habits; gambling is addictive through exogenous rewards; it is the designer's job to make game structures fit a balanced life, Miyamoto's "On a sunny day, play outside." Lens #97 Transformation, #98 Responsibility. PDF 481-483, 488. *Apply:* real money is the exogenous reward, so the app must never add a pull to return; it should want to be closed. The copy on the results screen shapes how the council talks about money afterwards.

## 2. The interface lenses as one checklist

For each screen state, in order: control (#53): does every input do the expected thing at once · the six arrows: where does the confusion sit · virtual interface (#55): what does each label say that the world does not · transparency (#56): when does the person notice the app · feedback (#57): need, want, should feel, want to feel, goal · the tenth of a second: acknowledged at once, confirmed later · juiciness (#58): how many replies per action · channels and dimensions (#59): the ranked information list and its map · modes (#60): is this phase unmistakable, does any control change meaning · sound as touch · a metaphor for the delay. PDF 253-275.

## 3. Where Schell warns against the patterns this product refuses

- Points nobody can use are ignored within minutes (Bubsy), PDF 64; a game with no value of its own needs real money to be played (roulette), PDF 63.
- Competition can cost more in hurt feelings than it gives in winning, and it broke up girls' play groups, PDF 134, 136; "because multiplay is cool" is not a reason, PDF 387.
- Flow needs rising challenge, and most players eventually give up in the frustration zone, PDF 151; designers who ramp difficulty to stretch play lose nine in ten, PDF 209; virtual skills pushed too far feel hollow, PDF 182-183.
- Rewards wear off, so games inflate and randomise them; Schell calls the trick cheesy and says it works even on those who see it, PDF 222. Punishment must be preventable or the game is unfair, PDF 225; reward beats punishment, PDF 224.
- Letting players tune their own challenge ends in boredom, "a little like trying to kick heroin", PDF 234.
- Exogenous rewards are what make gambling addictive; designers must fit games into a balanced life, PDF 481-482.
- "Is a game really the best solution?" is one of the problem-statement questions, PDF 93; the toy lens, not the game, is the one for something fun to play with rather than to win, PDF 121-122.

## 4. Not in this edition

- No Lens of Moments and no Lens of Primality (both are second-edition additions). Nearest: the hook and the climax of the interest curve, and cutting a flat part rather than filling it, PDF 279-282; empathy for faces and "fear minus death equals fun" for what reaches people below thought, PDF 143, 154.
- No chapter on onboarding; nearest is written rules giving way to the game teaching itself, PDF 177, the newbie level where learning to play is the game, PDF 394, and the gradual steps of a jigsaw, PDF 246.
- Nothing on trust in hidden mechanisms such as encryption; nearest is the hierarchy of knowers, PDF 169-170, and the consistency rule, PDF 307.

## 5. Audit questions

**Every screen, in this order**

1. Essential experience (#1): which of the six Game Design §1 principles does this screen carry, and does anything on it work against one? [PDF 51-52]
2. Toy (#15) and accessibility (#48): would a newcomer want to touch something before reading, and is the first step obvious? [121-122, 244]
3. Unification (#9): does every element belong in the burrow; which could be in any app? [80-84]
4. Pleasure (#17): which pleasure is this screen for; if none, it is a form. [140-143]
5. Feedback (#57): need, want, should feel, want to feel, goal, for each state including waiting and error. [261]
6. The tenth of a second: is every input acknowledged at once and the save confirmed later, quietly? [262-263]
7. Channels and dimensions (#59): what is always visible, what is a glance, what is hidden, and is that the ranking the moment needs? [265-269]
8. Modes (#60): can you tell the phase from a thumbnail; does any control change meaning between phases? [269-271]
9. Visual design as indirect control (#72): where does the eye land first, and is that the one action? [318-322]
10. Inner contradiction (#82): a quiet tool must not blink, shout or ask twice. [365-366]
11. Elegance (#43): count the purposes of each element; what can be removed? [228-229]
12. Dynamic state (#22): what is hidden from whom, and does the screen say so? [167-171]

**Moments**

- *Arrive (invite link):* the hook of the interest curve; a gateway that makes sense alone; what is this world better than? [279, 337-338]
- *Gathering (writing a statement):* expression (#85), the one place a person is heard before they are counted; does it say who will read it and when? [394, 169-170]
- *Reading statements:* discovery and curiosity (#4); the binocular effect, your own critter close before the grid. [61, 231]
- *Scattering:* control (#53), juiciness (#58), meaningful choice (#32) with the running split visible; constraints enforced by the heap, not error text; the reassurance that it can be changed until the close. [253, 264, 211-212, 179, 225]
- *Waiting:* visible progress (#49) and time (#39): how many have scattered, how long until the feast; a designed rest in the curve, not a dead end. [245, 220]
- *Feast:* the climax; freedom kept by *Skip to results*; no sad faces (empathy, status); nobody out-ranks anybody in the animation. [279, 226, 154, 354]
- *Results:* judgment (#20) must read as fair and not invite improvement; the non-scatterer notice as a preventable, quiet consequence; completion as the only reward; copy that shapes how the council speaks afterwards. [159, 225, 221, 482-483]
